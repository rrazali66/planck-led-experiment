Determination of Planck's Constant from the Threshold Voltages of Light-Emitting Diodes

Razif Razali · UTMSCNI, Universiti Teknologi Malaysia

Laboratory report · 17 October 2026

Abstract

The turn-on voltage of a light-emitting diode is measured for seven LEDs of known peak emission wavelength (465–944 nm). Because the photon energy at the emission threshold equals the junction energy gap, the reciprocal wavelength should be linear in the threshold voltage, with a gradient equal to e/hc. An unweighted least-squares fit to seven points gives a gradient of 786 885 ± 35 142 m−1V−1 (R2 = 0.9901), yielding h = (6.79 ± 0.30) × 10−34 J s — 2.50 % above the CODATA value of 6.62607015 × 10−34 J s, or 0.55 standard uncertainties. The value is therefore consistent with the accepted constant within experimental uncertainty. The fit returns a significant positive intercept (4.14 × 105 m−1, 7.35σ from zero) which the analysis shows is quantitatively explained by a constant threshold deficit of 0.476 ± 0.052 V between the measured turn-on voltage and Eg/e; forcing the regression through the origin is shown to be unjustified and would bias h low by 22 %.

1. Aim

To determine Planck's constant h from the variation of the threshold (turn-on) voltage V0 of light-emitting diodes with their emission wavelength λ, and to test the result against the accepted value. A secondary aim is to identify and quantify the systematic effects that limit the accuracy of the method.

2. Theory

An LED emits at a threshold voltage when the electrical energy delivered to each carrier, eV0, is just sufficient to generate a photon of the band-gap energy. Equating the electrical energy to the photon energy hc/λ,

eV0 = hν = hc/λ (1)

Rearranging into the form of a straight line,

1/λ = (e/hc) V0 (2)

so that a plot of reciprocal wavelength against threshold voltage should be a straight line of gradient

m = e/hc    ⇒    h = e/(mc) (3)

with e = 1.602176634 × 10−19 C and c = 2.99792458 × 108 m s−1. Equation (3) is the working equation of the experiment: h follows directly from the gradient measured in SI units, with no other calibration constant involved. For the CODATA value of h the expected gradient is e/hc = 806 554 m−1V−1.

Equation (2) requires the line to pass through the origin, since a photon energy of zero implies a threshold voltage of zero. A measured non-zero intercept is therefore a statement about the experiment, not about the physics, and is treated explicitly in Section 6 rather than removed by constraining the fit.

3. Apparatus and circuit

The apparatus comprised: a variable DC power supply (0–12 V); seven LEDs with nominal peak emission wavelengths spanning 465–944 nm; a protective series resistor; a digital ammeter and a high-impedance digital voltmeter; and a detector for the emitted light (see §4). Wavelengths are taken as the manufacturers' nominal peak values, which is itself a source of systematic uncertainty discussed in §7.

4. Method

  1. The supply was set to zero output and the circuit of Figure 1 assembled with one LED in position and the protective resistor in series.
  2. The supply voltage was increased slowly from zero while the emitted light was monitored. The voltage at which emission first became detectable was recorded as V0 for that LED.
  3. Step 2 was repeated for each of the seven LEDs, recording the peak wavelength and threshold voltage for each.
  4. The data were plotted as 1/λ against V0 and fitted by unweighted least squares to obtain the gradient m and its standard error, the intercept b, and the coefficient of determination R2. Planck's constant follows from h = e/(mc), with the relative uncertainty in h equal to that in m.
  5. The fit was not constrained through the origin. The intercept was retained and interpreted (§6), because constraining it would conceal precisely the systematic the experiment is meant to expose.
A note on the detection criterion

The threshold is defined here as the voltage at which emission first becomes detectable, which is a property of the detector as much as of the LED. This matters for two of the samples: the 943.5 nm and 878.7 nm emitters are in the near infrared, beyond the long-wavelength limit of human vision (roughly 700–750 nm even for a dark-adapted eye). Those two points cannot have been found by eye. They require a photodiode, a phototransistor, or an unmodified silicon camera sensor (which is sensitive to roughly 1100 nm). The detection method used for each LED should therefore be stated explicitly, since a change of detector between samples introduces exactly the wavelength-dependent threshold shift that Section 6 identifies as the dominant systematic.

5.2 Planck's constant

Substituting the fitted gradient into equation (3), with e and c taken as exact defined constants, gives

h = e/(mc) = (6.79 ± 0.30) × 10−34 J s (4)

Compared with the CODATA 2018 value h = 6.62607015 × 10−34 J s, the result is 2.50 % high. Expressed in units of the standard uncertainty this is a deviation of only 0.55σ. The correct conclusion is therefore that the measurement is consistent with the accepted value within experimental uncertainty — a 2.5 % difference is not, on this data set, evidence of a real discrepancy, because the uncertainty on the gradient is larger than the difference itself.

Reporting precision honestly

A percentage difference quoted without its uncertainty is meaningless. Quoting this measurement as agreeing to 2.19 % or 2.50 % implies a precision the experiment does not have: the standard error on the gradient is 4.47 %, so the honest statement is h = (6.79 ± 0.30) × 10−34 J s, i.e. “within about 5 % of the accepted value”, never “within 2 %”. The 2.19 % figure arises from comparing against a rounded value of h and from overlooking the fit uncertainty; it overstates the precision by roughly a factor of two.

6. The threshold deficit and the intercept

The fit returns a positive intercept, b = (4.140 ± 0.564) × 105 m−1, which is 7.35 standard errors from zero and amounts to 38 % of the range of the 1/λ data. Equation (2) requires b = 0, so this intercept is a real feature of the data that must be explained rather than ignored.

6.1 The physical origin: a constant threshold offset

Define the threshold deficit d as the shortfall of the measured turn-on voltage below the band-gap voltage,

d = Eg/e − V0   ⇒   Eg/e = V0 + d (5)

Substituting Eg = hc/λ into (5) and rearranging,

1/λ = (e/hc) (V0 + d) (6)

Equation (6) makes the structure of the problem clear. If d were a constant, the plot of 1/λ against V0 would still be a perfect straight line — but with gradient exactly e/hc and intercept (e/hc)d. The constant part of the deficit displaces the line vertically (producing the intercept) without tilting it. Only variation of d from LED to LED tilts the line and so corrupts the gradient, which is the quantity from which h is obtained.

Table 3 lists the deficit for each LED. The values are remarkably constant: d = 0.476 ± 0.052 V, a relative spread of only 11 %. The deficit does not trend with wavelength.

The ratio eV0/Eg rises from 0.648 at 943.5 nm to 0.840 at 502.6 nm — an apparent drift with wavelength that looks, at first sight, like a soft-knee systematic growing with photon energy. It is not. Because d is essentially constant while Eg grows by a factor of two across the set, the ratio eV0/Eg = 1 − d/(Eg/e) must rise with wavelength, and it does so by exactly the observed amount. The constant-deficit model in the final column of Table 3 reproduces the measured ratios to a mean absolute residual of 0.018. The apparent wavelength drift in the ratio is an artefact of dividing a constant offset by a gap energy that varies by a factor of two; it is not evidence of a wavelength-dependent deficit.

6.2 The intercept, quantified

Two independent routes give the same offset. Taking the mean of the directly measured deficits gives d̄ = 0.476 ± 0.052 V. Converting the fitted intercept into a voltage, b/(e/hc) = 0.513 V. These agree to 8 %, well within the combined uncertainty. The positive intercept is therefore quantitatively accounted for by the threshold deficit, and is not evidence of a fault in the measurement of h.

Why the fit must not be forced through the origin

It is tempting to impose b = 0 on the grounds that theory requires it. Doing so redistributes the intercept into the gradient: the constrained fit gives m = 1 033 489 m−1V−1 and hence h = 5.17 × 10−34 J s, a wholly incorrect 22 % below the accepted value. The origin constraint is not a physical improvement; it is an assumption that the data violate, and imposing it converts a well-understood systematic into a large error in the final answer. The intercept should be reported and explained, which is what distinguishes a measurement from a curve-fitting exercise.

7. Uncertainty analysis

7.1 Uncertainty budget

The relative uncertainty in h equals that in the fitted gradient (equation 3). Three contributions are identified. The statistical term is the standard error of the gradient. The wavelength term arises because the nominal peak wavelength of an LED may differ from its actual peak by of order 10–20 nm; at 600 nm a 15 nm error is 2.50 %. The deficit term is the measured tilt of the gradient: the fitted gradient is 786 885 against the 806 554 m−1V−1 demanded by e/hc, i.e. 2.44 % low, and §6 shows this entire tilt comes from the 11 % variation of d about its mean. It is quoted here as a systematic bound rather than as a random uncertainty.

Both statements support the same conclusion; the second is the more complete. In neither case is the difference from the accepted value significant.

Two conclusions follow, and the first is counter-intuitive. The near-infrared points are load-bearing, not detrimental. Removing them — the natural instinct, since they are the hardest to measure — destroys the result, giving 15.6 % high with a doubled uncertainty, because they are the only points sampling the low-V0 end and hence the only constraint on the lever arm. The two points that most degrade the fit are the mid-visible 591.7 nm and 502.6 nm LEDs, which carry the largest residuals (Table 1) and the largest excursions in the deficit d (Table 3: 0.564 V and 0.396 V against a mean of 0.476 V). Those are precisely the two points where the measured deficit departs furthest from the constant-offset model, and removing them yields h 0.14σ from the accepted value with R2 = 0.9986. That agreement is not the reported result — points may not be discarded for improving the answer — but it identifies with confidence exactly where the dominant systematic lives, and it is consistent with the §6 finding that variation in the threshold deficit, not wavelength, is what limits this experiment.

7.4 A caution against single-point determination

h may be computed from any one LED alone as h = eV0λ/c. The seven single-point values are 4.30, 4.46, 4.92, 4.84, 5.11, 5.56 and 5.50 × 10−34 J s — a spread of a factor 1.29 and every one of them low. Every such value is wrong specifically because it ignores the threshold deficit d of §6: using equation (6), a single LED gives h = e(V0 + d)λ/c, and dropping d ≈ 0.48 V is a large error. Only the gradient is robust to a constant offset, which is why the experiment is designed as a straight-line fit. Single-point values should not be quoted.

8. Discussion

The experiment determines Planck's constant to about 5 % and finds it consistent with the accepted value. That outcome is worth examining, because the naive reading of the same data — a gradient 2.5 % below e/hc — invites the wrong conclusion twice over: first, treating the 2.5 % as a discrepancy when it is well inside the uncertainty; second, treating the significant positive intercept as an embarrassment to be removed by constraining the fit, when it is in fact the most informative feature of the data set.

The analysis of §6 shows why the method works as well as it does. The threshold deficit d between the measured turn-on voltage and Eg/e is about 0.48 V and is substantially constant across a wavelength range over which the gap energy doubles. A constant offset moves the fitted line vertically and leaves its gradient untouched, so the quantity that determines h survives it. Only the residual 11 % variation of d about its mean tilts the gradient, and that tilt — 2.44 % — accounts for the whole of the deviation from the accepted value. This is a satisfying closure: the two systematics that could plausibly be blamed, the near-infrared detection and the wavelength accuracy, are quantified and neither dominates, while the effect that does dominate is measured directly from the data.

The practical implication is clear and cheap to implement. Because the accuracy of this experiment is limited by the variation of the threshold criterion between samples, the single most effective improvement is not better optics or more points but a better-defined threshold. Replacing “first detectable emission”, which is subjective and detector-dependent, with a fixed photocurrent criterion — for example the voltage at which the LED current reaches a stated value, or at which a photodiode behind the LED registers a stated output — would make d the same for every sample and remove the tilt at its source. Using a spectrometer to measure each LED's true peak wavelength rather than trusting the nominal colour would remove the second systematic. Both changes target the deficit, and both are within reach of a teaching laboratory.

Finally, the detector question raised in §4 deserves a direct answer in any write-up of this experiment. Two of the seven emitters cannot be seen by eye at all. If the 943.5 nm and 878.7 nm thresholds were established by eye, the numbers as recorded are not meaningful; if they were established with a silicon detector, that should be stated, along with whether the same detector and the same criterion were used for the visible samples. The consistency of d across all seven (Table 3) suggests a single consistent criterion was in fact used throughout, which is the outcome the analysis prefers — but the report should say so rather than leave it to be inferred.

9. Conclusion

The threshold voltages of seven LEDs of known peak wavelength were measured and fitted to 1/λ = m V0 + b. The gradient gives

h = (6.79 ± 0.30) × 10−34 J s   (random)    /   (6.79 ± 0.39) × 10−34 J s  (with systematics) (7)

which is 0.55σ (or 0.43σ) from the CODATA value of 6.62607015 × 10−34 J s. The measurement is therefore consistent with the accepted value of Planck's constant. A significant positive intercept (4.14 × 105 m−1) is shown to arise from a constant threshold deficit of 0.476 ± 0.052 V, whose 11 % variation accounts for the entire 2.44 % tilt of the gradient and hence for the whole of the difference from the accepted value. Constraining the fit through the origin would corrupt h by 22 % and is unjustified. The dominant limitation is the definition of the emission threshold, and specifying it by a fixed current or photocurrent rather than by first visible glow would materially improve the accuracy of the method.
